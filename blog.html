"In Defense of the Poor Image" by Hito Steyerl

Upon reading Hito Steyerl’s “In Defense of the Poor Image”, I immediately was reminded of the Marxist theory of dialectical materialism. From my understanding,
  dialectical materialism, a method in which to understand reality, poses that the material world is a result of dynamic processes and changes guided by internal contradictions.
  Steyerl’s “In Defense of the Poor Image”—I believe—argues a similar perspective; ‘the poor image’ reflects the contradictions between capitalist media productions and the
  proletariat reclamation of the same media. The poor image exists as a result and response to the neoliberal privatization of cinema and media. As cinema has become more
  difficult to access, ‘the poor image’grants nearly universal access to the world—past, present, and future. The digitization of media elucidates on the issue of accessibility
  of cinema and the Eurocentric ideals that are so prevalent in our society. Third Cinema and Tricontinentalist media are equated with the poor image; however, the poor image has
  given us access to nonconformist materials that challengethese societal constructs. 
Despite the poor image arising as a tool of the people, capitalist interest has coopted and commodified the medium. In the era of the attention economy and short attention spans,
  the accessibility of the poor image represents a gold mine of profits. The cyclicity of humanity is evident in ‘the poor image’; what was once for the people slowly becomes
  for the profits until a new medium for the people arises and the cycle renews again. Ultimately, as Steyerl states, the poor image “is about reality” (8).

"The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction" by Walter Benjamin

In “The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction”, Walter Benjamin explores and analyzes the effect of mechanical reproduction and capitalism on art and how it can be used
  as a tool for the masses. With the ability to easily reproduce images and art, art was becoming more distant from authenticity and less ritualistic. In the past, art was a practice
  rooted in tradition; whereas, in the present “mechanical reproduction emancipates the work of art from its parasitical dependence on ritual” (6). Delving deeper into mechanical
  reproduction, Benjamin discusses film and photography as the most radical mechanism of reproduction. Not only is the creation of art more accessible, but the participation in the
  consumption and criticism of art is more accessible. Reproducing and removing art from its original contexts, the authority of institutions and tradition dissipates. 
At the end of the essay, Benjamin argues that communism politicizes art while fascism aestheticizes politics. Benjamin provides Marinetti’s manifesto on the Ethiopian colonial war
  as an example of the fascist aestheticization of politics. In the manifesto, Marinetti describes war as beautiful through a futurist lens. Construing war and its subsequent
  violence as beautiful presents an ignorant perspective that dismisses the immense pain and suffering that war creates. It is because of these philosophies that Benjamin believes
  that art should be in the hands of the proletariat.

"As We May Think" by Vannevar Bush

Within the context of World War II, Bush claims that “this has not been a scientist’s war; it has been a war in which all have had a part” and so he poses the question: “What are the
  scientists to do next?” (112). In response, Bush details five machines that he believes are necessary for the progression of science: cyclops camera, microfilm, vocoder,
  thinking machine, and memex. Much of these inventions exist in some capacity today, and arguably, a modern-day phone or computer has the function of all five of these machines.
  Reading about these machines, I kept returning to the idea of AI and the concept of convenience. Ultimately, I understood Bush’s imagined machines as a tool of convenience.
  The purpose of these machines is to clear space in the human mind, so that we can conceive other things—much of what is marketed as the function of AI today. When thinking about
  AI and convenience, I often think about how AI did not always exist and I fear that the desire for convenience clouds our desires for true progress. Vannevar Bush writes
  “the applications of science have built him a well-supplied house and are teaching him to live healthily”, and yet our applications of science are now concentrated on discovering
  artificial general intelligence (124). 
After reading Vannevar Bush’s “As We May Think” and his accompanying bio, my main question lies in where Bush imagines the purposes of these machines he foresees. Acknowledging his
  position as the head of the Office of Scientific Research and Development, I wonder if he envisions these machines as a tool of war or science in general; or rather, is science
  primarily a tool of war? The same question can be applied to the machines we are witnessing being invented now. Is AI just another tool of war? Has science become intrinsically
  linked to the military industrial complex?
