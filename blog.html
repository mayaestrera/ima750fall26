9/8:

Upon reading Hito Steyerl’s “In Defense of the Poor Image”, I immediately was reminded of the Marxist theory of dialectical materialism. From my understanding,
  dialectical materialism, a method in which to understand reality, poses that the material world is a result of dynamic processes and changes guided by internal contradictions.
  Steyerl’s “In Defense of the Poor Image”—I believe—argues a similar perspective; ‘the poor image’ reflects the contradictions between capitalist media productions and the proletariat reclamation
  of the same media. The poor image exists as a result and response to the neoliberal privatization of cinema and media. As cinema has become more difficult to access, ‘the poor image’
  grants nearly universal access to the world—past, present, and future. The digitization of media elucidates on the issue of accessibility of cinema and the Eurocentric ideals that are so
  prevalent in our society. Third Cinema and Tricontinentalist media are equated with the poor image; however, the poor image has given us access to nonconformist materials that challenge
  these societal constructs. 
Despite the poor image arising as a tool of the people, capitalist interest has coopted and commodified the medium. In the era of the attention economy and short attention spans,
  the accessibility of the poor image represents a gold mine of profits. The cyclicity of humanity is evident in ‘the poor image’; what was once for the people slowly becomes
  for the profits until a new medium for the people arises and the cycle renews again. Ultimately, as Steyerl states, the poor image “is about reality” (8).

9/15:

In “The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction”, Walter Benjamin explores and analyzes the effect of mechanical reproduction and capitalism on art and how it can be used
  as a tool for the masses. With the ability to easily reproduce images and art, art was becoming more distant from authenticity and less ritualistic. In the past, art was a practice
  rooted in tradition; whereas, in the present “mechanical reproduction emancipates the work of art from its parasitical dependence on ritual” (6). Delving deeper into mechanical reproduction,
  Benjamin discusses film and photography as the most radical mechanism of reproduction. Not only is the creation of art more accessible, but the participation in the consumption and criticism
  of art is more accessible. Reproducing and removing art from its original contexts, the authority of institutions and tradition dissipates. 

At the end of the essay, Benjamin argues that communism politicizes art while fascism aestheticizes politics. Benjamin provides Marinetti’s manifesto on the Ethiopian colonial war as an example
  of the fascist aestheticization of politics. In the manifesto, Marinetti describes war as beautiful through a futurist lens. Construing war and its subsequent violence as beautiful presents
  an ignorant perspective that dismisses the immense pain and suffering that war creates. It is because of these philosophies that Benjamin believes that art should be in the hands of the proletariat.
